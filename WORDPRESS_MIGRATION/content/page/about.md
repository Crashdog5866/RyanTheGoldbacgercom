      <p>Ryan Goldbacher is a touring audio engineer, tour manager, and production manager. He's spent more than twenty years on the road, mixing front of house and running tours for artists across rock, punk, country, and heavy metal.</p>

      <section style="margin-bottom: 3rem;">
        <h2>First live sound</h2>
        <p>He didn't set out to do this. He was a guitarist who went to <strong>Charter Tech</strong>, a performing arts high school in New Jersey, because the academics were better than the local public option. He had to declare a major, and guitar was the only thing he had any real interest in, so that's what he picked. Then the school brought in music technology, and a teacher named <strong>Lew London</strong> — a jazz guitarist who played in the <strong>Bubba Mac Blues Band</strong> — noticed Ryan sitting in the music room every day, noodling on guitar while he waited for a ride home.</p>

        <p><strong>Lew</strong> called him over to a computer running an early version of Pro Tools and asked if he wanted to learn it.</p>

        <p><em>"That's a dumb name for a piece of software. What the heck is Pro Tools?"</em></p>

        <p>Then Ryan sat down with it and found something out. He couldn't play the way he wanted to. But he could listen. And he could use the DAW like a word processor — cut, delete, move things around, hear what changed. That was the click. It's the thing he's been chasing ever since.</p>
      </section>

      <section style="margin-bottom: 3rem;">
        <h2>The road</h2>
        <p>The touring career came together fast. <strong>Bill Anderson</strong> was early — a country legend who'd written more hits than almost anyone, and who told stories about <strong>Dolly Parton</strong> and <strong>George Strait</strong> and <strong>Little Jimmy Dickens</strong> like they were just people he knew. No pretense, no ego. Just another day at work, with legends in the room. Ryan learned from the way Bill talked about all of it — nothing was a big deal, and that was the whole point.</p>

        <p>Then <strong>Shooter Jennings</strong>. That one came sideways. Ryan was working at <strong>SIR</strong> as a rehearsal tech when <strong>Evanescence</strong>'s manager called him by mistake, thinking he was another engineer. Ryan corrected him, then said the thing that changed his career: I'm still interested in the gig. <strong>Andy</strong> called back a few days later and asked if Ryan had ever tour-managed. He hadn't. He said yes anyway.</p>

        <p>That first tour was the education of a lifetime, mostly because of everything he didn't know. He had no idea how to settle a show. He had no real understanding that not every promoter plays straight. He made mistakes early — the kind that cost real money — and had to learn the hard way that this job is equal parts logistics, people skills, and knowing when to push back.</p>

        <p>Everything after came from that first yes. <strong>Gavin DeGraw</strong>, <strong>Bayside</strong>, <strong>The Record Company</strong>, <strong>Stiff Little Fingers</strong>, <strong>Black Star Riders and Thin Lizzy</strong>, <strong>Samantha Fish</strong>, <strong>Accept</strong>, <strong>Mr. Big</strong>, <strong>The HU</strong>, <strong>Pop Evil</strong>, and now <strong>Tom Keifer</strong>. Country, punk, hard rock, folk-metal, legacy acts. He worked as tour manager, production manager, FOH engineer, and monitor engineer — sometimes all of them on the same run. The genre never mattered as much as whether the show happened and sounded right.</p>
      </section>

      <section style="margin-bottom: 3rem;">
        <h2>What he actually believes about mixing</h2>
        <p>Ryan has a specific view of what the job is. It's not to impress other engineers. It's not to show off gear. It's not to chase a spec sheet.</p>

        <p><em>"There's two people I'm trying to impress every night — myself and the guy signing my check in the middle of the stage."</em></p>

        <p>A good mix is tight. It's cohesive. It sounds like a band playing in a room, not like a pile of tracks fighting each other. When he hears a front-of-house mix that puts everything on the kick drum and nothing on the rest of the band, he calls it out plainly — that's not a mix, that's a drum solo with accompaniment.</p>

        <p>His technical philosophy follows from the same place. <em>"Never think of the kick drum as a bass drum. It's a very full range instrument."</em> He doesn't load the sub with kick. He lets the bass do the low-end lifting, on purpose, and keeps the kick punchy in the range where it actually reads. It's a small distinction that changes how a whole band sits together.</p>

        <p>And when someone asks him for a rule: <em>"There's no rules for any of this. My opinion shouldn't matter that much unless it's going to get you fired. Turn the knobs till it sounds good to you."</em></p>
      </section>

      <section style="margin-bottom: 3rem;">
        <h2>Leadership</h2>
        <p>Early in his management career, Ryan had what he now calls a <em>"very immature idea of what a leader should be."</em> <em>"I was the boss. I was, 'Do this. You don't get to have a say.'"</em></p>

        <p>The road corrected him. On a bus, across time zones, under pressure, you can't just give orders — you have to build trust. Letting people solve problems their own way, and trusting them to be good at their jobs, works better than any structure he could impose. That's the advice he gives young tour managers now. Not the logistics — the leadership.</p>
      </section>

      <section style="margin-bottom: 3rem;">
        <h2>The work now</h2>
        <p>Ryan currently mixes FOH for <strong>Tom Keifer</strong> and runs tour and production management for a rotating roster of artists. He's also the voice behind <strong>@RyanTheGoldbacher</strong>, where he makes short-form videos about the craft and about the industry itself — kick drum technique, why a mix has to be tight before it can be loud, why the sound of a console matters more than its spec sheet, what it actually takes to survive a career on the road. He's a vocal advocate for the working engineer: proper pay, proper hospitality, and proper respect for the crew that makes the show happen.</p>

        <p>He still believes the best way to learn this job is to be in the room with people who know more than you do, and the clearest signal of a professional is how they treat the people around them.</p>
